# The auction and what a click costs

## Bid and price

- A bid is the maximum an advertiser authorises per click, or per 1,000 viewable impressions. (B1)
- Amazon describes "a form of generalized “second-price” auction" with a hard reserve a bid must exceed to enter and soft reserves set in real time. (B1)
- In Amazon's description, a winner above both reserves pays the soft reserve; a winner above the hard reserve but below the soft one still wins and pays its own bid. (B1)
- Amazon states that "the price charged to you may exceed the runner-up bid". (B1)
- Amazon states that reserves move with factors including predicted sale likelihood, predicted return on ad spend, competing bids, placement, context and peak periods, and "aren't predictable in advance by anyone, including Amazon or the advertiser". (B1)
- Amazon states that it never charges more than the maximum effective bid, which includes bid adjustments and the bidding strategy. (B1)
- Chuck Kessler (Canopy Management) quotes Amazon's wording that the final CPC "will never exceed your maximum adjusted bid" and holds that the gap between bid and average CPC is the advertiser's only account-specific read on the price cushion. (B1)
- Amazon ranks ads by relevance and bid and states that ads with lower bids and higher relevance "can and do win". (B2)

## The FTC case

- The FTC and 22 states allege, in a complaint of 2026-08-31 that is untested, with trial set for 2027-03-29, that an undisclosed soft reserve "beginning in 2019" made Sponsored Products advertisers pay their own bid 30–40% of the time in 2021, 70% in 2022 and about 80% in 2024. (B3)
- The complaint also alleges that Amazon raised reserve-price surcharges during Prime Day and other high-velocity events. (B3)
- It alleges that Amazon more than doubled top-of-page desktop ads in one year although an internal study found higher ad loads "decreases purchase rates and increases search abandonment". (B3)
- Texas, suing separately the same day, alleges soft reserves lift the winner's cost by roughly 17% on ordinary days and more than 25% at peak events. (B3)
- Amazon disputes the characterisation. (B3)
- Amazon states that inflation-adjusted SP search CPC was flat from 2019 to 2024 while conversion rates rose 24% from 2021 to 2025. (B3)
- Amazon states that about 92% of selected SP ads in 2024 were not the highest bid, and that competitors' average bids on keywords fell about 83% in one study when one advertiser stopped bidding. (B3)

## Auction research

- In auction research, reserve prices raised a search engine's revenues "substantially" in a large field experiment. (B4)
- In the generalized second-price auction a bidder's price per click depends on the bids and quality of the advertiser ranked below it. (B4)
- Researchers at Amazon Ads and Outreach describe a soft floor under which a bidder still wins and pays its own bid. (B4)
- In their simulation, soft floors barely change revenue with symmetric single-query bidders but can change it when bidders choose which queries to target. (B4)
- Ad Badger holds that an advertiser can infer its auction pricing only from its own account, as average CPC over the current bid per target, with ratios near 0.90–1.00 meaning effectively first-price. (B4)

## Position

- Published web-search studies disagree on how conversion rate moves with ad position: highest at the top (Ghose & Yang), raised by higher positions (Rutz, Bucklin & Sonnier), rising lower down (Agarwal, Hosanagar & Smith, who also find profit can be higher below the top); none of the four is on Amazon. (B21)
- Narayanan & Kalyanam's regression-discontinuity design finds naive position comparisons biased by auction selection, and causal effects much smaller, present only at some positions, larger for smaller advertisers and less experienced consumers, and weaker for brand- or product-specific keywords and exact match. (B21)
- Click-model research finds click probability depends on position apart from relevance. (B21)
- A field experiment found more rival ads above the organic results raised the top sponsored listing's click-through rate. (B21)
- Walmart's and Amazon researchers' sponsored-products systems treat position as a confounder and de-bias for it. (B21)
