## What this covers

This file covers the money side of a seller's ad work: what an ad sale is worth to the seller, what the seller is trying to get from advertising, and which limits outside the ad console (fees, margins, stock) bound what advertising can sensibly do. It covers:

- **Break-even ACOS and break-even ROAS**, as Amazon defines them, and the margin before ad cost they rest on;
- **how that margin is computed** from Amazon's fee schedule, the Selling Partner API's fee estimates and the seller's own costs, and which Amazon costs a per-unit fee estimate does not carry;
- **target ACOS and ROAS**, and how practitioners derive a target from a profit goal;
- **TACoS** (ad spend over total sales) and how it relates to ACOS and to profit after ads;
- **the economics of a click**: CPC, conversion rate, order value, cost per acquisition, and what a click actually costs under Amazon's auction (including the reserve-price facts that became public in the August 2026 FTC complaint and Amazon's response);
- **marginal against average returns**: why the last dollar of spend can lose money while the campaign's average ACOS looks fine;
- **goals**: profit, growth, launch, defence and liquidation as Amazon and practitioners frame them; the console's and the API's goal, KPI and bid-strategy fields; Amazon's guidance on choosing an objective;
- **lifetime value and repeat purchase**: new-to-brand metrics, Amazon's long-term sales metric, Subscribe & Save, the Repeat Purchase report, Amazon's "affordable CPA";
- **inventory as a limit on ad spend**: out-of-stock pausing, FBA capacity limits and the IPI, storage and aged-inventory costs, days of supply and weeks of cover, and the reports that carry them;
- **dated fee changes** (2026 and 2027 US letters, the fuel surcharge, holiday peak fees), **returns** as a margin input and what attributed sales deduct, **Amazon Points** on Amazon.co.jp as a seller-funded price lever, and **price against advertising** as levers on the same sales (sections C, O, P, Q; added in the gap fill);
- **practitioners' frameworks**, named, and where they disagree;
- **research** on advertising elasticity and optimal ad spend (Dorfman–Steiner, the advertising-elasticity meta-analyses, TV and paid-search ROI studies, LTV of search-acquired customers, paid-to-organic spillover), on why a seller's own regression does not identify the elasticity and what experiments do, and on price elasticity.

Why a senior Amazon advertising lead cares. Every other decision in the pack (a bid, a budget, a harvest, a pause, a season plan) is judged against a number: a target ACOS, a ROAS floor, a TACoS ceiling, a cost per new customer. If that number is wrong, every downstream change is wrong in the same direction. The commonest failures a lead sees are:

- a break-even computed from cost of goods alone, leaving out referral and FBA fees;
- one target for a whole catalog with very different margins;
- an average ACOS used where the marginal one decides;
- attributed sales treated as incremental sales;
- a launch judged against a mature product's target;
- spend scaled into a stock-out.

This file gives the model the definitions, Amazon's own rules and the measured effects needed to set and read those numbers. It sets none of them.

## Amazon's own rules and guidance

Each claim is marked **RULE** (Amazon states it as a requirement or definition, an API enforces or exposes it, or it is a fee or limit) or **GUIDANCE** (Amazon recommends or illustrates it).

### A. ACOS, ROAS and break-even, as Amazon defines them

- **RULE (definition).** "Amazon ACOS is calculated by dividing ad spend by ad revenue, then converting it to a percentage." "ACOS = (ad spend ÷ ad revenue) x 100". "ROAS = ad revenue ÷ ad spend". "Return on ad spend (ROAS) is the inverse of Amazon ACOS". Example: "if you spent $50 on an ad campaign and earned $100 from it, your Amazon ACOS would be 50%" and "the ROAS would be 2". [official] *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)? Calculation and tips | Amazon Ads*, https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/acos-advertising-cost-of-sales
- **RULE (definition, other page).** The budget guide says the same with "ad-attributed revenue": "You calculate ACOS by dividing your ad spend by your ad-attributed revenue"; "A lower ACOS indicates a better performance when you are measuring success based on ad-attributed sales." [official] *Advertising budget: Definition, examples, measurement*, https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/advertising-budget
- **RULE (break-even).** "Break-even ACOS is directly linked to your profit margin. In order to maintain a profit, your Amazon ACOS needs to be lower than your profit margin. Otherwise, you will be spending more on ads than you're earning." The same page defines profit margin loosely: "it's the amount earned beyond the cost of product production and any other expenses your brand may have." [official] *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?*, as above.
- **GUIDANCE (target).** "Each brand is different and will have a different target ACOS. However, the first step should be achieving break-even ACOS and comparing it to your profit margin. From there, you can determine what goal is most important to your brands and campaigns: Is it increasing sales? Is it raising brand awareness?" [official] *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?*
- **GUIDANCE (no universal number).** "There isn't a definitive number for a good Amazon ACOS. It's dependent on your industry, company size, and campaign frequency, among other variables." [official] same page.
- **GUIDANCE (newness).** "new campaigns tend to have a high ACOS, merely because of their newness. But that doesn't make a new campaign a failure; it means you should consider different KPIs." [official] same page, section *Why you shouldn't focus too much on ACOS*.
- **RULE (API definitions).** The v1 reporting metric `metric.roas` is "Return on ad spend, calculated as attributed sales per unit of ad spend. ROAS = sales / total cost", eligible for Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display, Sponsored TV, DSP and Full-Funnel. `metric.totalCost` is "The total amount spent on running the campaign. This excludes the third-party fees paid by the agency." [official] *Purchases (Amazon retail conversions)*, https://d3a0d0y2hgofx6.cloudfront.net/en-us/guides/reporting/ads-v1/metrics/amazon-retail-conversions/purchases.md; *Costs (Costs and fees)*, https://d3a0d0y2hgofx6.cloudfront.net/en-us/guides/reporting/ads-v1/metrics/costs-and-fees/costs.md
- **RULE (API, v3).** Reporting v3 carries a precomputed `acosClicks7d` ("Advertising cost of sales based on purchases made within 7 days of an ad click") and `acosClicks14d`, `roasClicks7d` and `roasClicks14d`, in the `spTargeting`, `spSearchTerm` and `spAdvertisedProduct` report types (among others); `cost` is "Total cost of ad clicks"; `sales7d` is "Total value of sales occurring within 7 days of an ad click". The v3 page also splits same-SKU from other-SKU sales: `attributedSalesSameSku7d` ("where the purchased `SKU` was the same as the `SKU` advertised") and `salesOtherSku7d` ("where the purchased `SKU` was different"). [official] *Reporting columns (version 3)*, https://d3a0d0y2hgofx6.cloudfront.net/en-us/guides/reporting/v3/columns.md. The same page opens with the banner "Reporting API v3 will be deprecated on June 30, 2027." and points to the v1 reporting API (date for human readers only). Which window applies to which ad product is `beyond/metrics-and-attribution.md`'s.

[seat] Amazon's break-even rule compares ACOS, an attributed-sales ratio, with a margin. Two consequences follow from the definitions, not from any Amazon page:

1. If attributed sales include other SKUs (`salesOtherSku7d`), the margin that matters is the margin of the mix that sold, not of the advertised SKU.
2. If some attributed sales would have happened without the ad, an ACOS under break-even does not by itself mean the ad paid for itself (section N; *Published research*; `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`).

### B. Ads Math: break-even ROAS, the click-to-sale chain, and lifetime value

Amazon's *Ads Math* guide is the most complete statement of unit economics on any Amazon Ads page read. It is "a chapter in the Ads Strategy Guidebook for holiday marketing 2025". [official] *Practical calculations for holiday marketing success | Amazon Ads* (Ads Math), https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/ads-math

- **GUIDANCE (no threshold).** "There is no general threshold for good or bad return on ad spend (ROAS). A "good" ROAS is one that is profitable for you. Start by identifying your break-even ROAS based on your profit margin. This tells you the minimum ROAS you need to not lose money."
- **GUIDANCE (ROAS is preliminary).** "ROAS alone is a preliminary metric. It should be considered in the context of your profit margin. A high ROAS may still mean you're losing money if your margin is too low. To answer questions like "is this good or bad?", preliminary metrics should be interpreted in context and paired with deeper performance indicators—such as conversions, customer lifetime value (LTV), profit, or incremental lift."
- **RULE-shaped formulas (Amazon's).**
  - "(Revenue – Cost of goods sold) ÷ Revenue = Gross profit margin"
  - "1 ÷ Gross profit margin = Break-even ROAS"
  - "A break-even ROAS of 2.5 means you need $2.50 in attributed sales for every $1.00 spent on ads to break even."
  - "If campaign ROAS greater than or equal to break-even ROAS, then the campaign is profitable (or at least not losing money)."
- **GUIDANCE (worked table, figures for human readers only).** Advertiser A: ROAS 3, margin 20%, revenue $3,000, gross profit $600, ad spend $1,000, net −$400. Advertiser B: ROAS 2, margin 60%, revenue $2,000, gross profit $1,200, ad spend $1,000, net +$200. "Advertiser A has a higher ROAS, but because their margin is small, they still lose money."
- **GUIDANCE (bidding up).** "Seasonal events may lead to higher cost per click (CPC) but often increase conversion and average order value (AOV) too. It's essential to model the full equation to see if the trade-off is worth it." Example (figures for human readers only): "CPC is projected to increase by 25%. ROAS is expected to drop from 3.2 to 2.8. Your break-even ROAS is 2.0." Verdict: "The campaign is still profitable because a ROAS of 2.8 is greater than 2.0 (break-even point). Therefore, you can afford to bid up." Key insight: "Go in with a plan, not fear. Test and scale where the math supports it."
- **RULE-shaped formulas (the click chain).** "Don't judge CPC alone as it is a preliminary metric. Link it to conversion rate and profit per order to find your effective cost per acquisition (CPA)". The formulas:
  - "CPC ÷ Conversion rate = CPA"
  - "Average order value × Gross profit margin = Profit per sale"
  - "Profit – CPA = Net profit or loss per sale"
  - and: "If the net profit or loss per sale result is positive, then the campaign is profitable. If it's negative, then the campaign is unprofitable, and you either need to lower your CPC or increase your conversion rate."
  - The page limits this calculator: it "is meant to determine if your CPC is too high in relation to CPA for one-off purchases and does not take into account customers who continue to purchase from your brand."
- **RULE-shaped formulas (lifetime value).**
  - "To set a profitable ad budget, you first need to calculate customer lifetime value (LTV). Then, using your profit margin, you can determine your affordable cost per acquisition (CPA)—the maximum you can spend to acquire a customer and still break even over their lifetime."
  - "Average order value × Average orders = Customer LTV"
  - "LTV × Profit margin = Affordable CPA"
  - "Once you've identified your affordable CPA, you can use it as the upper limit for your ad spend per new customer to ensure you break even over their lifetime."
  - "It helps you avoid common pitfalls like bidding too low on valuable audiences or scaling back campaigns that are profitable in the long run, even if their initial return seems low."

[seat] Three things about Ads Math a skill should hold precisely:

1. Its "gross profit margin" is revenue minus cost of goods sold, over revenue. For a seller on Amazon, Amazon's own selling and fulfillment fees (section C) are costs that scale with each unit sold. A margin that leaves them out overstates break-even ACOS by the fee share of price. The page does not say whether "cost of goods sold" is meant to include them.
2. The LTV formula multiplies order value by order count and then by "profit margin". It does not discount future orders, and it does not deduct a repeat order's own fees or a Subscribe & Save discount. Those are a seller's own choices to add.
3. The bid-up example compares the *projected average* ROAS after the change with break-even. The ROAS of the *added* spend alone can be lower than the new average (section N; *Published research*, Shapiro, Hitsch and Tuchman).

**Algebra that follows from Amazon's own definitions** [seat, an identity, not a claim of Amazon's]:

- ACOS = spend ÷ sales = (clicks × CPC) ÷ (orders × AOV) = CPC ÷ (CVR × AOV), where CVR = orders ÷ clicks and AOV = sales ÷ orders.
- Break-even CPC (Amazon's "net profit per sale = 0") = AOV × margin × CVR.
- Therefore ACOS < margin and "Profit – CPA > 0" are the same test for one-off purchases.
- "AOV" here is attributed sales per attributed order, and "CVR" is attributed orders per click. Both are read from the same report, with the same window.

[seat] Per-unit costs that scale with a sale fall into two groups:

- Costs Amazon charges per unit: referral fee, closing fee where it applies, FBA fulfillment fee.
- Costs that are per unit only after allocation: storage, inbound placement, returns processing, aged inventory, a seller-funded Subscribe & Save discount, coupon and deal fees.

The second group is real but depends on stock levels, sell-through and the seller's choices. That is where practitioners differ on what "margin before ads" includes (*What practitioners add*).

[official] *analytics_economics_2024_03_15.graphql*. MCF fulfils "customer orders when they buy on your own website or another sales channel" [official] *Costs for optional selling services | Sell on Amazon*, https://sell.amazon.com/pricing/optional-costs; Amazon's MCF page describes "a single pool of inventory" serving "all your sales channels—Amazon, DTC, social platforms, and more" [official] *Multichannel Fulfillment (MCF)*, as above. An Amazon-store order is fulfilled by FBA at FBA fees, so MCF fees are not a cost of an Amazon ad sale. They enter only through the shared pool (peak storage, capacity).

[seat] What the dated fee changes do to break-even:

- Break-even ACOS is margin before ads over price. A per-unit fee change of Δ moves break-even ACOS by Δ ÷ price, in percentage points. For the same Δ the move is largest on low-priced items.
- The holiday peak fulfillment fee (October 15 to January 14 in 2026–27) and the peak monthly storage rate coincide with the weeks when CPCs and deal fees peak (`areas/seasons-and-deals.md`). A break-even computed on non-peak fees overstates the peak-season break-even.
- The 3.5% surcharge is a percentage of the fulfillment fee, so it scales with every fulfillment-fee change, peak included.
- Storage utilization surcharge and aged-inventory surcharge are holding costs, not per-sale costs, until 2027; from February 1, 2027 Amazon folds the aged surcharge into an age-based monthly rate. For a skill, the per-unit storage share stays an allocation (section K) whatever the rate card.
- A price change that crosses a fulfillment-fee price band (per PPC Land's reading of the rate cards, $10 and $50) changes the fulfillment fee as well as the referral fee. A price cut is therefore not a pure margin-for-volume trade at the band edges (section Q).

### E. What a click costs: bids, reserve prices and the 2026 FTC complaint

The CPC in the click chain (section B) is what the seller pays, not what the seller bids. How the two relate became a matter of public record in 2026.

- **RULE (bid is a ceiling).** "Sponsored Products uses a cost-per-click , auction-based pricing model. You set the maximum amount that you are willing to pay when a customer clicks an ad for your product." "A bid is the maximum amount you are willing to pay when a customer clicks an ad." [official] *FAQs - Advertising questions and resources*, https://advertising.amazon.com/resources/faq
- **RULE (what goes into ad selection).** "When choosing Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Products , Amazon Ads primarily considers the keywords advertisers bid on, the amount advertisers bid, and the customers' expected interest in advertiser offers, based on information about the offer, similarities to other offers, and past customer purchases." "Sponsored ads products do not charge any "demand-side fees"". [official] *Your guide to Amazon Ads pricing transparency*, https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/pricing-transparency
- **RULE (Amazon's description of its pricing, 2026).** Amazon's published response to the FTC lawsuit states:
  - "Since we first introduced ads in our Store in 2006, we have priced clicks using a form of generalized "second-price" auction".
  - "we began to test a concept called "soft reserve prices," a real-time minimum value that seeks to better reflect what each placement is actually worth. We also introduced what we call a "hard reserve," which is the minimum a bid must surpass to enter an auction."
  - "When the winning advertiser's bid exceeds both the hard and soft reserve, they pay the soft reserve, which is less than they were willing to pay. When the winning advertiser's bid exceeds the hard reserve but doesn't meet the soft reserve, we still grant the placement to that advertiser and they pay their bid. In no scenario does an advertiser pay more than their bid."
  - "reserves are determined in real time and aren't predictable in advance by anyone, including Amazon or the advertiser."
  - "The Ad Console Campaign Builder ... has clearly stated since 2018 that a bid represents the maximum an advertiser could be charged."
  - "we updated our Amazon Ads Help content to explicitly explain the use of reserve prices."

  [official] *Amazon's response to the FTC's lawsuit regarding Sponsored Ads*, https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-ftc-sponsored-ads-lawsuit-response (the HTML URL returned HTTP 404 to a plain GET and to WebFetch at verification on 2026-11-09; the text was read from Amazon's markdown copy at https://www.aboutamazon.com/md/company-news/amazon-ftc-sponsored-ads-lawsuit-response)
- **RULE-adjacent (Amazon's figures, for human readers only).** Same page: "in 2024, approximately 92% of selected Sponsored Products ads were not the highest bid"; "The mean winning advertiser's bid is typically about the 128th bid by amount"; "from 2019 to 2024, the average winning bid for Sponsored Products search ads fell 50%"; "for Sponsored Products search ads, from 2019 through 2024, the average cost-per-click to advertisers remained flat when adjusted for inflation"; "Conversion rates ... have increased over 24% for individual Sponsored Products advertisers from 2021 through 2025"; "eighty percent of bid changes on clicked Sponsored Products search ads occurred within one day of a prior change". [official] same page.
- **The allegation (not a finding).** The FTC press release, dated August 31, 2026, says Amazon told advertisers it ran "second price" auctions where winners would pay "one cent more than the next highest bidder". It alleges that by 2024 advertisers paid their own winning bid about 80% of the time on Sponsored Products ads, up from 30–40% in 2021 and 70% in 2022, after Amazon from 2019 added what it internally called a "soft reserve price". The suit is in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington and covers "Sponsored Product ads," "Sponsored Brands ads," and "Display Ads." [government; not Amazon's words] *FTC, States Sue Amazon over Secret Ad Surcharge Scheme*, https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme (read through the WebFetch tool; a plain GET returned no article text). Nothing in the complaint has been tried.
- **RULE (dynamic bidding raises the ceiling).** In the Ads API v1 `BidStrategy` enum: `SALES_UP_AND_DOWN` "Increases or decreases your bids in real time by a maximum of 100%"; `SALES_DOWN_ONLY` "Bids will never increase beyond your set bid"; `MANUAL` "Uses your exact bid and any placement adjustments you set, and is not subject to dynamic bidding." [official] *Amazon Ads API v1 merged OpenAPI* (`AmazonAdsAPIALLMerged_prod_3p.json`), https://d1y2lf8k3vrkfu.cloudfront.net/openapi/en-us/dest/AmazonAdsAPIALLMerged_prod_3p.json. The exact ceilings per placement, and placement multipliers, are `areas/bids-and-placements.md`'s.

[seat] For the economics, the click chain uses the *paid* CPC from a report (`cost` ÷ `clicks`, or `costPerClick`). Under Amazon's own description:

- the paid CPC equals the bid whenever the bid is under the soft reserve;
- the soft reserve is unknowable in advance;
- the "max bid = value per click" rule from second-price theory is therefore not a safe assumption about what a bid will cost;
- the ratio of paid CPC to the effective bid (after dynamic and placement adjustments) is observable per target, from the seller's own reports.

[seat] Several Amazon numbers point at the value of a customer beyond the first order, and they are not the same thing:

- `costPerNewToBrandPurchase` divides *all* cost by NTB purchases. It is not the cost of the NTB purchases alone.
- Long-term sales is a modelled forecast. It covers SB, SD, ST, DSP and Full-Funnel, but not SP.
- Ads Math's "affordable CPA" is a seller-side calculation from the seller's own LTV.
- The Repeat Purchase report and the Replenishment API (section I) are observed seller-side history.

A skill treating any one of these as "LTV" needs to know which one it is reading.

### J. TACoS in Amazon's words

- **GUIDANCE (definition).** "Monitor your Total Advertising Cost of Sales (TACOS) to understand how efficiently your advertising investment is working. TACOS shows your ad spend as a percentage of total sales." [official] *The author's guide to Sponsored Products* (datePublished 2023-09-07, dateModified 2026-10-09), https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/authors-guide-to-sponsored-products. It is written for authors (KDP).
- **GUIDANCE (the wider view, a second Amazon page; restated in the gap fill).** "ACOS only measures the efficiency of ad-attributed sales, not the full picture. That's where total advertising cost of sales (TACOS) comes in. TACOS reveals whether your total sales (organic and paid) are growing in relation to your ad spend." The page then quotes an agency (Adam Mellott, BTR Media, [opinion]): "When ACOS rises during high-traffic events, we don't immediately pull back. Instead, we look at TACOS—and often, we find it dropping." [official, with an [opinion] quote] *Holiday advertising myths: What experts want you to know* (datePublished 2025-09-26, dateModified 2026-10-09), https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/common-holiday-advertising-myths
- So two Amazon library pages name TACOS as ad spend against total sales, total sales being "organic and paid". Neither names the sales field, the ad products counted or the window. No Ads API metric named TACoS was found in the v1 metric families or v3 columns read. This matches the owner file's settled wording (`areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`, P-ads-organic-and-total-6).
- [seat] TACoS needs total sales from outside the Ads API: Sales and Traffic (`orderedProductSales` from `GET_SALES_AND_TRAFFIC_REPORT`, section *What cx must read at run time*) over the same days and the same marketplace. It also needs ad spend summed across every ad product that advertises the ASIN or brand. Identity: TACoS = ACOS × (attributed sales ÷ total sales). With one margin m for every unit, profit after ads as a share of total sales = m − TACoS. Both identities hold only if every unit carries the same margin.

[seat] Inventory bounds advertising in four ways the model can read:

1. **No stock, no ad.** The ad stops serving.
2. **Selling faster than stock can be replaced.** This brings forward a stock-out. Its costs are the lost sales and the ranking effects owned by `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`, and, where the fee applies, the low-inventory-level fee flagged in the planning report.
3. **Holding too much.** This costs storage, aged-inventory surcharges and IPI, and through IPI it costs capacity. Here ad spend that moves units has a value beyond the sale's margin: the storage and surcharge it avoids.
4. **Capacity limits.** These cap what can be restocked. Demand created beyond capacity cannot be served from FBA.

The arithmetic links ad-driven units to days of cover: days of supply shrink by the extra units per day. That is what makes "weeks of cover" and an ad budget the same decision. No Amazon page read here states a rule linking ad spend to stock cover; Amazon's own planning report gives the cover, and the ad reports give the units.

### N. Where Amazon's pages disagree, or are silent

1. **What "profit margin" means.** The ACOS guide's margin is "the amount earned beyond the cost of product production and any other expenses". Ads Math's margin is "(Revenue – Cost of goods sold) ÷ Revenue". The FBA page says to "calculate your margins by taking storage and fulfillment costs into account". The three are not stated as the same quantity.
2. **Benchmarks.** The ACOS guide ("There isn't a definitive number") and Ads Math ("no general threshold") against the FBA guide ("A good benchmark is earning at least $2–4 for every $1 spent").
3. **Average against marginal.** Ads Math's bid-up example tests the projected average ROAS. No Amazon page read here discusses the ROAS of incremental spend. Marketing ROI suggests "subtracting organic sales growth", and Ads Math lists "incremental lift" among deeper indicators, but neither gives a method for Sponsored Products. Incrementality tools are `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`'s.
4. **Goals for SP.** Sponsored Brands has goal-based campaigns. The v1 entity guide gives SP no goal settings, while the v1 enums and the Ads Agent news describe goal-led buying across products. The status of an SP ROAS-auto-bidding beta is reported only by a practitioner.
5. **Long-term value metrics skip SP.** NTB purchases are SP-eligible in v1 but absent from v3 SP report types. Long-term sales, NTB ROAS and S&S subscriptions are not SP-eligible in v1.
6. **TACoS.** Named on two Amazon library pages (the author's guide gives the ratio; the holiday myths page gives the "organic and paid" denominator); neither names fields, and no API metric was found.
7. **Subscribe & Save funding.** The same sell.amazon.com page states Amazon's 5% two ways (on "orders of five items or more", and as a flat Amazon-funded 5% on auto-enrolled products).
8. **Auction pricing.** Amazon's description (soft reserve paid when the bid exceeds it, otherwise the bid) and the FTC's allegation (own bid paid ~80% of the time) are both public. The case is untried.
9. **Returns.** Sponsored-ads attributed sales deduct only payment failures and cancellations within 72 hours (section O); Amazon's economics dataset nets refunds out of product sales. The two Amazon "sales" figures a margin can be built on differ by exactly the returns.
10. **Points (JP).** Amazon's pricing model subtracts points from the landed price used in its competitive comparisons, while the seller pays for them as a cost on top of the sale (section P). No Amazon page read says whether ad-attributed sales subtract points.

[seat] What returns do to the ad arithmetic:

- Attributed sales are gross of returns, so ACOS read from an ad report is spend over gross sales. With a return rate r on refunded revenue, spend over kept revenue is ACOS ÷ (1 − r). For r = 0.2 a 30% ACOS is 37.5% of kept revenue.
- A returned FBA unit also costs whatever the seller cannot recover: a returns processing fee where it applies, an unsellable disposition, and fees Amazon does not refund. Margin before ads per kept unit falls on both sides of the ratio.
- Data Kiosk's return figures lag about 45 days and are mapped to the ship date, so the last six weeks' return rate reads low; the economics dataset counts refunds "by refund request time". A return rate measured in either needs a window that ended at least the lag ago.
- The return rate that matters for an ASIN is that ASIN's (Seller Analytics `returnedRate`), not the account's (Sales and Traffic `SalesByDate`).

### Q. Price and advertising as two levers on the same sales (added in the gap fill)

- **GUIDANCE (Amazon: price and stock gate the ad).** "your product must be in stock and priced competitively in order to become the Featured Offer, so take into account product pricing and availability when deciding to advertise." [official] *FAQs*, section K. The Featured Offer comparison uses landed price (section P).
- **GUIDANCE (Amazon: deals and ads together).** "Products with deals or coupons that were advertised with Sponsored Products saw an average 65% increase in sales and 75% increase in units sold compared to when the deal or coupon product was not advertised" (figures for human readers only). [official] *Which products should I add to my campaign?*, section F. Amazon's comparison is advertised against not advertised for a deal product, not a price cut against a bid raise.
- **RULE (coupons and attributed sales).** "Attributed sales don't reflect any discounts applied in the cart." [official] *Ad campaign attribution*, section O. [seat] A discount applied in the cart leaves attributed sales at the pre-discount price, so ACOS read during a cart-applied promotion is spend over a revenue the seller did not receive.
- **RULE (Amazon tests prices with switchbacks).** Amazon's pricing scientists write that "all visitors to the Amazon Store at the same time see the same prices for all products — we need to apply experimental treatments to product prices over time"; random-days switchbacks reduce standard error "by 60%", and carryover arises because "if treatment increases a product's sales, recommendation algorithms may recommend that product more often". [research, Amazon-authored] Cooprider and Nassiri, *The science of price experiments in the Amazon Store* (Amazon Science, April 14, 2023), https://www.amazon.science/blog/the-science-of-price-experiments-in-the-amazon-store
- [seat] The Dorfman–Steiner condition already sets the two levers against each other (*Published research*): with the price at its own optimum (margin = 1 ÷ |price elasticity|), ad spend over sales = advertising elasticity ÷ |price elasticity|. The ratio of the two elasticities, not either alone, says how much of a sales goal is cheaper to buy with ads than with price. A price cut also moves the referral fee, can move the FBA fee band (section C), changes the Featured Offer comparison, and changes the conversion rate in the click chain; a bid raise changes CPC and placement. Both move sales rank where rank follows sales (`areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`).

### From a profit goal to a campaign target

- **My Amazon Guy** (as above) builds the target in three steps:
  1. margin before ads;
  2. minus a target net margin ("A net margin of 15% to 30% is a typical range for Amazon FBA sellers"), giving an "ideal true ACoS" (their name for TACoS: "Is true ACoS the same as TACoS? Yes");
  3. "Divide your ideal true ACoS by the share of total sales that comes from ads."

  Example: a 38.3% margin before ads minus a 28% target net margin leaves about 10.3%. "The same product supports a 29.5% campaign ACoS when ads drive 35% of sales, but only 17.2% when ads drive 60%." Identity: "Your profit after ads equals your margin before ads minus your true ACoS". Per-ASIN targets: "Set a target for each ASIN based on its own margin and stage, then combine them into one brand number weighted by sales."
- **Perpetua** (help centre; author Ashley Chung per the page's metadata), *Sponsored Products Goal Setup: Targeting Types, Target ACoS, and Budget*, https://help.perpetua.io/en/articles/4859772-sponsored-products-goal-setup-targeting-types-target-acos-and-budget. Perpetua sets targets from history, not from margin:
  - "Look at your historical ACoS and budget over the past 30 days to set your initial targets."
  - For brand-based goals: "Your brand name is less expensive, so it should have a lower budget and target ACoS than the unbranded segment."
  - For advanced goals: "Competitor keywords and ASINs are more expensive so they should have a higher ACoS than the branded & category segments."
  - "Minimum $10 daily budget".
- **Where they differ.** My Amazon Guy derives the target from margin and profit goal. Perpetua anchors it on the realised ACoS of the last 30 days and varies it by segment (brand, competitor, category). The two can give very different numbers for the same product.

### Disagreements a skill should know exist

1. **What margin before ads includes.** Returns and storage allowances: My Amazon Guy yes, EightX's worked example no. Amazon's Ads Math names only cost of goods.
2. **Launch tolerance.** Launch ACOS or TACoS bands differ by firm (15–25% to 25–40%, "up to 50%", ACoS "40–50%").
3. **Target source.** Margin-derived (My Amazon Guy, EightX) or history-derived (Perpetua).
4. **Whether rank justifies over-break-even spend.** Canopy, EightX, AdLabs and My Amazon Guy say a launch or ranking phase justifies it. Amazon's pages say new campaigns have high ACOS "merely because of their newness" (section A) and do not tie it to rank. The rank mechanism is `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`'s.
5. **Where returns go.** Practitioners read put returns into margin before ads as an allowance (Nova, FiveX, My Amazon Guy). Amazon leaves them in attributed sales (section O). [seat] Break-even ACOS on gross attributed sales is right only if the allowance covers what a return loses (the refunded price less what is recovered, plus fees and processing), not the processing cost alone.

### Optimal advertising: the Dorfman–Steiner condition

- **What it says.** "The optimal level of advertising for a firm is found where the ratio of advertising to sales equals the price-cost margin times the advertising elasticity of demand. The obvious result is that the greater the degree of sensitivity of quantity demanded to advertising and the greater the margin on the extra output then the higher the level of advertising." Source: Dorfman, Robert, and Peter O. Steiner (1954), "Optimal Advertising and Optimal Quality", *American Economic Review* 44, 826–36. [research] *Dorfman–Steiner theorem* (Wikipedia, last edited 2023-08-23), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorfman%E2%80%93Steiner_theorem. The original article was not read (JSTOR not tried).
- **Transfer** [seat]. Written with Amazon's own quantities, the condition is: ad spend ÷ total sales = m × ε. Here m is the margin before ads (price minus marginal cost, over price) and ε is the elasticity of *total* sales with respect to ad spend. Three consequences follow by algebra:
  1. The left side is TACoS, not ACOS: the condition is about all sales the spend moves.
  2. The condition is a marginal one. It is equivalent to m × (dS/dA) = 1: the last dollar of spend returns exactly 1/m in total sales, which is Amazon's break-even ROAS applied to the *marginal* dollar.
  3. Because measured advertising elasticities are well below 1 (next subsection), m × ε is a small fraction of m. A TACoS equal to the margin (all margin spent on ads) is far above the profit-maximising level whenever ε is small.

  Limits: the model is static (one period, no carry-over); it takes the elasticity as known and constant; and it ignores ranking dynamics, capacity, and the auction.

- **Why a seller's own regression does not give ε (added in the gap fill).** The condition needs the *causal* elasticity. A seller regressing sales on its own spend gets a biased one, because spend is set with knowledge of demand. Budgets are raised for peaks, cut at stock-outs, and Amazon's own dynamic bids move "in proportion to the likelihood of a conversion" ([official] *Bidding strategies for Sponsored Products*, https://advertising.amazon.com/help/GCU2BUWJH2W3A8Z7, re-read 2026-11-09). The research says this in so many words:
  - Shapiro, Hitsch and Tuchman (2021): "In general, advertising is not randomly assigned, and thus, in the presence of unmeasured confounders, the estimated advertising effects do not have a causal interpretation." Their identification "relies on the specific institutions of the ad buying process". "As much as 80% of advertising is purchased in an upfront market well in advance of the ads being aired." They assume "brands cannot observe" local, transient demand shocks "at the time of purchasing an ad spot". As a robustness check they use "changes in advertising at TV market borders", which "requires that consumers who are exposed to advertising do not cross the border and purchase in the neighboring DMA", and find the border results "nearly identical" to the baseline. With that identification "the mean and median of the distribution of estimated long-run own-advertising elasticities are 0.023 and 0.014, respectively, and two thirds of the elasticity estimates are not statistically different from zero." [research] the article's PDF, *Econometrica* 89(4) 1855–1879, read via a public copy, https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2021-shapiro.pdf (ligatures are lost in the text extraction; quotes restored).
  - Lewis and Rao (2015): "selection bias, due to the targeted nature of advertising, is a crippling concern for widely employed observational methods." [research] as below; abstract via OpenAlex, https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/qje/qjv023
  - Gordon, Zettelmeyer, Bhargava and Chapsky (2019), "A Comparison of Approaches to Advertising Measurement: Evidence from Big Field Experiments at Facebook", *Marketing Science* 38(2) 193–225: "Unobservable factors make exposure endogenous". Over "15 U.S. advertising experiments at Facebook comprising 500 million user-experiment observations", "The observational methods often fail to produce the same effects as the randomized experiments, even after conditioning on extensive demographic and behavioral variables." [research] EconPapers record, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:38:y:2019:i:2:p:193-225
  - Gordon, Moakler and Zettelmeyer (2022/2023), "Close Enough? A Large-Scale Exploration of Non-Experimental Approaches to Advertising Measurement": across "663 large-scale experiments at Facebook" with "over 5,000 user-level features", double/debiased machine learning and stratified propensity score matching both fail: "The median RCT lifts are 29%, 18%, and 5% for the upper, middle, and lower funnel outcomes, respectively. Using DML (SPSM), the median lift by funnel is 83% (173%), 58% (176%), and 24% (64%)"; "we are unable to reliably estimate an ad campaign's causal effect." [research] arXiv 2201.07055, https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.07055
- **The experimental route.** Randomising the spend removes the selection problem:
  - On a single seller's account, switching ads on and off by day. Jonathan Hershaff (a seller writing on his own Substack, February 13, 2026) did it "alternating my ads "On" and "Off" daily for about a month (post-Christmas late Dec 2025 – Jan 2026)". He reports a "Platform ROAS: ~2.35" against an "Incremental ROAS: 1.25" from the on/off difference in total sales, and calls 1.25 "likely my performance floor" because "A daily switchback assumes marketing has zero memory". He then fits an MMM (Google Meridian) to his switchback data for carryover. [opinion, one seller's account, figures for human readers only] *Estimating Amazon Ad Incrementality for Sellers: How to Combine Switchback Tests with MMM*, https://whatstheimpact.substack.com/p/estimating-amazon-ad-incrementality. Owner of the method: `method/change-measurement.md`; owner of the incrementality reading: `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`.
  - Amazon's experimenters use the same design family. Masoero, Missault, Delbé, Richardson and Imbens (2025), "Regular balanced switchback designs for robust multi-unit online experimentation": "a fixed fraction of items receive treatment at each time step, and all items receive treatment equally" over time; "40-60% reduction in standard errors compared to simple item randomization and 20-30% improvement over unbalanced switchback approaches", "unbiased estimation of average treatment effects under carryover effects". [research, Amazon-authored] https://www.amazon.science/publications/regular-balanced-switchback-designs-for-robust-multi-unit-online-experimentation
  - Jain, Hut, Islam and Pan (2023), "Cross-unit spillovers in A/B testing: Empirical evidence from ads": "in the context of advertiser experiments, a treatment may affect the outcome of control advertisers who compete with treated advertisers in ad auctions. Such spillover effects lead to biased treatment effect estimates under simple advertiser randomization due to the violation of the required Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA)." [research, Amazon-authored] https://www.amazon.science/publications/cross-unit-spillovers-in-a-b-testing-empirical-evidence-from-ads. [seat] For one seller's own on/off test, the analogue is the seller's own campaigns competing with each other and with the seller's organic listing, so the measured lift is the account's, not a campaign's.
  - Lewis and Wong ("Incrementality Bidding and Attribution", below) put randomisation inside bidding itself.
- **Transfer** [seat]. ε in the condition is a causal, total-sales, marginal elasticity. A regression of a seller's sales on its own spend estimates none of the three: spend follows demand, attributed sales are not total sales, and an average over a period is not a margin. Small ε also means Lewis and Rao's noise point applies: an on/off test on one account measures the lift with a wide band.

### How large advertising effects are: elasticity meta-analyses and large-scale ROI studies

- **Sethuraman, Tellis and Briesch (2011).** "How Well Does Advertising Work? Generalizations from Meta-Analysis of Brand Advertising Elasticities", *Journal of Marketing Research* 48(3). Meta-analysis of 751 short-term and 402 long-term brand advertising elasticities from 56 studies, 1960–2008. Findings:
  - mean short-term elasticity 0.12 (against 0.22 in the prior meta-analysis);
  - a decline over time;
  - elasticity higher for durable than non-durable goods, higher "in the early phase of the life cycle rather than in maturity", higher with annual than quarterly data, and higher when advertising is measured in GRPs than in money;
  - mean long-term elasticity 0.24 (prior 0.41).

  [research] IDEAS/RePEc record hal-02055301, https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/journl/hal-02055301.html. That record is the French-language version of the article ("À quel point la publicité est-elle efficace? ...", *Recherche et Applications en Marketing*, 2011, DOI 10.1177/076737011102600204); its abstract carries the figures above. The JMR article's own page was not read (see *Unreachable*). Transfer [seat]: mostly offline brand advertising on aggregate sales, not search ads on a marketplace. The life-cycle finding is the one an Amazon launch argument can cite: the same spend moves sales more for a new product than a mature one.
- **Shapiro, Hitsch and Tuchman (2021).** "TV Advertising Effectiveness and Profitability: Generalizable Results From 288 Brands", *Econometrica* 89(4), 1855–1879. Abstract: "Our results reveal substantially smaller advertising elasticities compared to the results documented in the literature, as well as a sizable percentage of statistically insignificant or negative estimates." "The ROI analysis shows negative ROIs at the margin for more than 80% of brands, implying over‐investment in advertising by most firms. Further, the overall ROI of the observed advertising schedule is only positive for one third of all brands." [research] EconPapers record, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:emetrp:v:89:y:2021:i:4:p:1855-1879. Transfer [seat]: TV, not search. But the study measures the gap this file cares about: ROI *at the margin* against ROI of the *whole schedule*. A schedule can be profitable on average while its last increment loses money. The 80%/one-third split shows the gap is common, not theoretical.
- **Blake, Nosko and Tadelis (2015).** "Consumer Heterogeneity and Paid Search Effectiveness: A Large-Scale Field Experiment", *Econometrica* 83, 155–174 (NBER w20171). Abstract: "returns from paid search are a fraction of conventional non-experimental estimates. As an extreme case, we show that brand-keyword ads have no measurable short-term benefits. For non-brand keywords we find that new and infrequent users are positively influenced by ads but that more frequent users whose purchasing behavior is not influenced by ads account for most of the advertising expenses, resulting in average returns that are negative." [research] NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w20171. Transfer [seat]: eBay on Google and Bing, for a famous brand. The parts that carry over are mechanisms, not magnitudes:
  - attributed is not incremental;
  - the incremental part sits with new and infrequent buyers, which is the economic reason a new-to-brand metric matters;
  - average return can be negative while some segments are positive.

  Measuring this in a seller's account is `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`'s and `method/change-measurement.md`'s.
- **Lewis and Rao (2015).** "The Unfavorable Economics of Measuring the Returns to Advertising", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(4), 1941–1973. Twenty-five field experiments: "The median confidence interval on return on investment is over 100 percentage points wide"; "a coefficient of variation of 10 is common"; "informative advertising experiments can easily require more than 10 million person-weeks"; "selection bias, due to the targeted nature of advertising, is a crippling concern for widely employed observational methods." [research] EconPapers record, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:4:p:1941-1973. Transfer [seat]: a single seller's estimate of the return on a spend change is noisy. A target ACOS is itself an estimate with a wide band, which is relevant to how hard a model can lean on a small difference from target.
- **Simonov, Nosko and Rao (2018).** "Competition and Crowd-Out for Brand Keywords in Sponsored Search", *Marketing Science* 37(2), 200–215. On Bing: "When no competitors are present, we find a positive, statistically significant impact of brand ads of 1%–4%, with larger brands having a smaller causal effect. In this case, the effective "cost per incremental click" is significantly higher than what focal brands typically pay on other keywords." When the brand does not advertise and competitors do, competitors "steal" 18%–42% of clicks, "Under such position effects, we find the return on investment on defensive advertising to be strongly positive." [research] EconPapers record, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:37:y:2018:i:2:p:200-215. Transfer [seat]: the economics of a branded term depend on whether competitors bid on it. The "cost per incremental click" idea (spend ÷ incremental clicks, not ÷ all clicks) is the general tool. Branded-term decisions are `beyond/brand-defense-and-competition.md`'s.

### Price elasticity, the other lever (added in the gap fill)

- **Bijmolt, Van Heerde and Pieters (2005).** "New Empirical Generalizations on the Determinants of Price Elasticity", *Journal of Marketing Research* 42(2) 141–156. "Across a set of 1851 price elasticities based on 81 studies, the average price elasticity is −2.62." "over the past four decades, sales elasticities have significantly increased in magnitude"; "accommodating price endogeneity has a strong (magnitude-increasing) impact on price elasticities." [research] Crossref record with abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmkr.42.2.141.62296. Transfer [seat]: set against the advertising elasticities above (means of 0.12 short-term in the meta-analysis, 0.023 in Shapiro et al.), the price elasticity is one to two orders of magnitude larger. In the Dorfman–Steiner ratio (section Q), that is why the profit-maximising ad-to-sales ratio is small.
- **Chevalier and Goolsbee (2003; NBER w9085, 2002).** "Measuring Prices and Price Competition Online: Amazon and Barnes and Noble". From sales ranks of about 20,000 books: "demand at Barnes and Noble is much more price-elastic than is demand at Amazon." In the paper: "the own price elasticity of demand is close to -4 and the cross-price elasticity very high" at BN.com, "than at Amazon where the price elasticity is around -0.6"; with one parameter choice "At Amazon ... it is actually less than one in absolute value, at -0.45", which the authors call "important and puzzling". [research] NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w9085 (abstract; the paper's PDF read for the figures). Transfer [seat]: books in 2001, Amazon as the retailer, not a third-party seller's single offer among rivals. It shows that Amazon-store demand can be far less price-elastic than a rival store's. That does not hold for every offer.
- **Bach, Chernozhukov, Klaassen, Spindler, Teichert-Kluge and Vijaykumar (2024).** "Adventures in Demand Analysis Using AI", arXiv 2501.00382. On "toy cars on Amazon.com", text, image and tabular embeddings "lead to more credible causal estimates of price elasticity"; "we uncover strong heterogeneity in price elasticity driven by these product-specific features." [research] https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00382. Transfer [seat]: price elasticity on Amazon varies by product within one category, so a category figure is not an ASIN's.
- **Amazon's own price testing.** Amazon runs price experiments as switchbacks because one product has one price at a time (section Q; Cooprider and Nassiri, Amazon Science, 2023).
- **Transfer** [seat]. No paper read here measures, for one Amazon listing, both its price elasticity and its advertising elasticity. Those are the two numbers the price-against-bid comparison needs.

### Value beyond the first order

- **Chan, Wu and Xie (2011).** "Measuring the Lifetime Value of Customers Acquired from Google Search Advertising", *Marketing Science* 30(5), 837–850. "Results show that customers acquired through Google search advertising in our data have a higher transaction rate than customers acquired from other channels. After accounting for future purchases and spillover to off-line channels, the calculated value of new customers using our approach is much higher than the value obtained using conventional method." Data: one small US firm, individual-level panel. [research] EconPapers record, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:30:y:2011:i:5:p:837-850. Transfer [seat]: supports Amazon's Ads Math point that judging acquisition by first-order return undervalues it when customers repeat. The size of the effect is one firm's.

### Paid-to-organic spillover through rankings

- **Ju, Zhao and Aral (2025).** "Advertising Spillovers in Mobile Apps: Evidence from Ad Shutoffs and Store Rankings", arXiv 2504.16151. A global ad shutoff by a mobile game developer "decreased organic installs by 20-30%"; "every $100 spent is associated with 32 paid installs and 2.2 organic installs"; "paid installs boost app store category rankings, thereby increasing organic visibility"; "the relationship between organic installs and ad spend disappears once these rankings are factored in". [research] https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16151. Transfer [seat]: a marketplace whose ranking uses sales could show the same mechanism. If it does, the return on ad spend includes organic sales the ads produce through rank, and that changes the margin-only break-even. Whether and how much Amazon's search behaves this way is `areas/ads-organic-and-total.md`'s.

### Evidence from Amazon's own data

- **Pauwels, Peran, Shah, Schnaidt and Vercamer (2023).** "Sponsored brands video rings up clicks and sales in the short and long run", *Applied Marketing Analytics*; listed on Amazon Science. Using Amazon.com data and causal-inference methods: "brands that invested in Sponsored Brands video (SBv) ads in addition to sponsored ads static ads had a 25% higher click-through rate (CTR) and 10% higher year-over-year sales growth". [research] Amazon Science, https://www.amazon.science/publications/sponsored-brands-video-rings-up-clicks-and-sales-in-the-short-and-long-run
  - **Identification (read in the gap fill).** The paper is observational. Brands chose whether to adopt SBv; nothing was randomised. For the month horizon the authors "use a two-stage Gaussian process ... (our version of causal multi-task Gaussian processes)" on "a matched sample of 25,364 advertisers in North America and Europe", finding that "brands that launched an SBv campaign for the first time obtained an average 21.3% increase in sales the following month compared with those that did not". For the year horizon "we applied propensity score stratification (Austin 2011) to compare sales growth and CTR". The adjustment uses "the 50+ features we used to account for confounding. The top confounders include the vertical (product category) of the advertiser, the number of enabled campaigns, trailing retail performance metrics, country of origin, and inventory position." The CTR comparison restricts to "consumers who each had both static and video impressions during the same month". Their limitations: "we did not analyze the role of frequency of video ad exposure, nor the impact on advertising efficiency, i.e. Return on Advertising Investment (ROAS)". [research] the paper's PDF, https://cdn.amazon.science/d1/66/f222c8524c4d87fc1b0b342345b7/sponsored-brands-video-rings-up-clicks-and-sales-in-the-short-and-long-run.pdf
  - **Transfer** [seat]. The estimates are causal only if adoption is as good as random once the 50+ observed features are matched. Brands that start video may differ in ways the features miss, such as a new product, a creative budget, or an agency. Gordon et al. (2019; 2023, above) found matching and propensity methods far from experimental results on Facebook ads even with thousands of features. The figures are therefore associations under selection on observables, from Amazon-authored work, about format mix and sales growth. They are not a ROAS and not an incrementality measurement.
- **Dash, Ghosh, Mukherjee, Chakraborty and Gummadi (2024).** "Sponsored is the New Organic: Implications of Sponsored Results on Quality of Search Results in the Amazon Marketplace", arXiv 2407.19099. From 4,800 simulated searches in four countries: "items with poor organic ranks (beyond 100th position) appear as sponsored results even before the top organic results on the first page"; "in majority of the cases, these top sponsored results are costlier and are of poorer quality than the top organic results." [research] https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19099. Transfer [seat]: confirms that ad position is available to products with no organic standing. That is the economic premise of launch spend.

### Bidding, diminishing returns and incrementality

- **Kong, Shmakov and Yang.** "Do not Waste Money on Advertising Spend: Bid Recommendation via Concavity Changes", arXiv 2212.13923. "The recommended bid is derived based on the turning point from significant increase (i.e. concave downward) to slow increase (convex upward)" of the click-prediction curve. [research] https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.13923. Transfer [seat]: the bid → clicks curve flattens, so each extra cent of bid buys fewer extra clicks. This is the mechanism behind marginal ACOS rising above average ACOS as bids rise.
- **Lewis and Wong.** "Incrementality Bidding and Attribution", arXiv 2208.12809. They propose a method that unifies "ad buying/bidding/pricing, attribution, and experimentation" so bids reflect "advertising's causal effects". [research] https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12809. Transfer [seat]: the value per click that should enter a bid is the *incremental* value. Amazon's attributed sales are an upper bound on it, not an estimate of it.
- **Li, Yuan, Yang, Chen and Song (2026).** "Attributed, But Not Incremental: Cannibalization-Corrected Attribution for Large-Scale Advertising", arXiv 2606.26690. "paid-attributed conversions ... may systematically overstate true incremental growth when paid channels overlap with organic demand, brand-driven traffic, or other acquisition channels"; their experiment-calibrated correction, deployed across "multiple global TikTok markets", "supported budget and traffic strategy adjustments that were followed by an approximately 15-percentage-point reduction in the measured cannibalization rate". [research] https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26690. Transfer [seat]: a different platform, the same structural point about attributed and incremental.

### What the research does not settle

[seat] No paper read here measures, on Amazon Sponsored Products:

- the elasticity of a seller's total sales to SP spend;
- the marginal ROAS of a bid increase;
- the share of attributed SP sales that is incremental.

The research gives mechanisms (diminishing returns, attributed ≠ incremental, value beyond first order, ranking spillover, early-life-cycle elasticity) and orders of magnitude from other channels. The numbers for a given seller can only come from that seller's own data and tests.

## Stable enough for a skill

Each item is an Amazon definition, an Amazon rule or API shape, an identity that follows from Amazon's definitions, or a measured effect with its source. Figures stay out of skill text and are read at run time.

1. ACOS is ad spend over attributed sales; ROAS is its inverse; Amazon's break-even rule is that ACOS must be under the profit margin, and break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ margin. (*What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?*; *Ads Math*; v1 *Purchases* `metric.roas`.)
2. Amazon's click chain: CPA = CPC ÷ conversion rate; profit per sale = AOV × margin; a one-off sale is profitable when profit per sale exceeds CPA. This is the same test as ACOS < margin. (*Ads Math*.)
3. Amazon's lifetime rule: customer LTV = AOV × average orders; affordable CPA = LTV × margin; Amazon calls affordable CPA "the upper limit for your ad spend per new customer". (*Ads Math*.)
4. Amazon's guides say there is no universal good ACOS or ROAS, and say new campaigns run high ACOS "merely because of their newness". One Amazon FBA guide nonetheless prints a $2–4 per $1 benchmark. (*ACOS* guide; *Ads Math*; *How to Help Increase Sales with FBA and Advertising*.)
5. Amazon's fees that scale with a sale are the category referral fee (a percentage with a minimum, on the total price including shipping and gift wrap), a media closing fee, and the FBA fulfillment fee based on price, weight and dimensions. FBA also charges storage, aged inventory over 181 days, returns processing, inbound placement and removal or disposal. (*Selling on Amazon fees*; *Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA)*.)
6. The SP-API Product Fees API returns an estimate at a price the caller supplies, per SKU or ASIN, with `TotalFeesEstimate` and a `FeeDetailList`. Amazon says the estimates "are not guaranteed", and ASIN estimates use catalog size. The FBA Fee Preview report gives current per-SKU fee estimates, refreshed at least every 72 hours and requestable once a day. Actual charges are in the Finances API. (*Product Fees API*; *productFeesV0.json*; *FBA Reports*; *finances_2024-06-19.json*.)
7. A bid is the maximum a click can cost. Amazon states that when the bid exceeds the soft reserve the winner pays the soft reserve, and when it does not the winner pays the bid. The FTC alleges advertisers paid their own bid about 80% of the time by 2024. (*FAQs*; Amazon's FTC response; FTC press release.)
8. In Ads API v1, Sponsored Products campaigns carry no goal settings. Sponsored Brands campaigns carry a read-only goal and a required KPI. The v1 `Goal`, `KPI` and `BidStrategy` enums are read from the model. (*The campaign entity*; v1 merged OpenAPI.)
9. Sponsored Brands has two goals: drive page visits (clicks, CPC) and grow brand impression share (top-of-search impression share, vCPM). (*Sponsored Brands goal-based campaigns*.)
10. SP rule-based bidding applies a ROAS guardrail that Amazon does not guarantee and disables under a stated condition; eligibility needs campaign age, recent conversions and a minimum daily budget. (*Sponsored Products rule-based bidding overview*.)
11. New-to-brand means no purchase from the brand in the prior 12 months. In v1, NTB purchases and sales are SP-eligible, but NTB ROAS is not. In v3, NTB columns appear in no SP report type. `costPerNewToBrandPurchase` divides total cost by NTB purchases. (v1 *Purchases (new to brand)*; v3 columns.)
12. Long-term sales (attributed sales plus estimated 365-day value of new-to-brand engagements) and long-term ROAS exist for SB, SD, ST, DSP and Full-Funnel, not for SP. S&S subscription metrics are likewise not SP-eligible. (v1 *Long-term sales*; v1 *Subscribe & Save subscriptions*.)
13. Seller-side repeat-purchase history is in the Brand Analytics Repeat Purchase report, which needs Brand Registry. Subscribe & Save history, including subscriber lifetime value by segment, retention and out-of-stock losses, is in the Replenishment API. (*Analytics Reports*; *Replenishment API*; model.)
14. An ad does not display when the product is out of stock or not the Featured Offer. (*FAQs*.)
15. FBA capacity limits depend on IPI and sales; usage counts on-hand plus inbound units; limits can be raised through Capacity Manager at a seller-set reservation fee. The IPI counts excess (over 90 days of supply), sell-through, stranded inventory and in-stock rate of popular products. (Amazon staff forum posts, 2024-03-14 and 2026-08-13.)
16. Amazon's inventory planning and restock reports give per-SKU days of supply, weeks of cover, excess quantity, aged-inventory exposure, low-inventory-level fee flags, seasonality flags and recommended actions. (*FBA Reports*.)
17. Brand advertising elasticities average about 0.12 short term and 0.24 long term, and are higher early in the product life cycle. TV ad ROI is negative at the margin for most brands even where the average schedule is profitable for some. (Sethuraman, Tellis and Briesch 2011; Shapiro, Hitsch and Tuchman 2021.)
18. Paid-search field experiments find attributed returns far above causal returns, with the incremental effect concentrated on new and infrequent buyers. (Blake, Nosko and Tadelis 2015; Lewis and Rao 2015.)
19. The Dorfman–Steiner condition sets the profit-maximising ratio of ad spend to sales at margin × advertising elasticity. This is equivalent to a marginal ROAS of 1 ÷ margin on total sales. (Dorfman and Steiner 1954, via the Wikipedia statement; the equivalence is algebra.)
20. Amazon announces US fee changes yearly, with most taking effect January 15. The 2027 letter (November 5, 2026) also has February 1, 2027 changes: the storage utilization surcharge ends, the aged-inventory surcharge merges into an age-based monthly storage rate, and the low-inventory-level fee changes its test. Holiday peak fulfillment fees run October 15 to January 14 (2026–27). A 3.5% fuel and logistics surcharge on fulfillment fees has applied since April 17, 2026. (2026 and 2027 fee letters; `News_Amazon` posts.)
21. Sponsored-ads attributed sales deduct payment failures and cancellations within 72 hours, and do not reflect cart discounts. No Amazon page read says returns are deducted. (*Ad campaign attribution*.)
22. Per-ASIN return rates are in Data Kiosk Seller Analytics (`returns.returnedRate`, mapped to ship date, about 45 days late) and can be built from the FBA customer returns report and the seller-fulfilled returns reports. Sales and Traffic gives refunds by date only. (Data Kiosk schemas and guide; *FBA Reports*; *Returns Reports*; Sales and Traffic schema.)
23. Data Kiosk's economics dataset gives per-ASIN or per-SKU net proceeds after refunds, Amazon fees, ad charges and seller-entered unit costs. It splits fees at an Amazon fee-change date and previews fees up to 120 days ahead. (*analytics_economics_2024_03_15.graphql*.)
24. On Amazon.co.jp, seller-set points cost the seller one yen per point and are set in Points Central (maximum 50%). Amazon's pricing model subtracts points from landed price in its competitive-price comparisons. (JP `News_Amazon` posts; JP Points terms; Product Pricing model.)
25. Observational estimates of ad effects (matching, propensity scores, DML), even with thousands of features, often miss experimental results. Spend chosen with knowledge of demand biases a seller's own regression of sales on spend. (Gordon et al. 2019; Gordon, Moakler and Zettelmeyer 2023; Lewis and Rao 2015; Shapiro, Hitsch and Tuchman 2021.)
26. Measured price elasticities (meta-analytic mean −2.62) are one to two orders of magnitude larger than measured advertising elasticities. Elasticities vary strongly by product on Amazon. (Bijmolt, Van Heerde and Pieters 2005; Bach et al. 2024.)

## Positions

**P-goals-and-economics-1.** Amazon defines ACOS as ad spend divided by ad-attributed revenue, times 100, and ROAS as ad revenue divided by ad spend, the inverse of ACOS. Sources: *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/acos-advertising-cost-of-sales); *Advertising budget* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/advertising-budget). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-2.** Amazon states that to maintain a profit, ACOS must be lower than the profit margin, and calls this break-even ACOS. Source: *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/acos-advertising-cost-of-sales). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-3.** Amazon's Ads Math gives gross profit margin as (revenue minus cost of goods sold) divided by revenue, break-even ROAS as 1 divided by that margin, and treats a campaign as not losing money when its ROAS is at or above break-even ROAS. Source: *Ads Math* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/ads-math). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-4.** Amazon states there is no general threshold for a good ACOS or ROAS, that a good ROAS is one that is profitable for the advertiser, and that ROAS is a preliminary metric to be read with margin, conversions, lifetime value, profit or incremental lift; one Amazon FBA guide nonetheless prints a benchmark of $2–4 per $1 of ad spend. Sources: *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?*; *Ads Math*; *How to Help Increase Sales with FBA and Advertising* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/fba). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-5.** Amazon's click-chain formulas are CPA = CPC ÷ conversion rate, profit per sale = average order value × gross profit margin, and net profit per sale = profit per sale − CPA; Amazon says this calculator is for one-off purchases and ignores repeat customers. Source: *Ads Math* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/ads-math). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-6.** Amazon defines customer LTV as average order value × average orders and affordable CPA as LTV × profit margin, and calls affordable CPA the upper limit for ad spend per new customer to break even over the customer's lifetime. Source: *Ads Math* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/ads-math). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-7.** Amazon states that new campaigns tend to have a high ACOS because of their newness, and that this does not make them failures but calls for other KPIs. Source: *What is advertising cost of sales (ACOS)?* (https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/acos-advertising-cost-of-sales). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-8.** Amazon's referral fee for each item is a category-specific percentage of the total price (list price plus shipping and gift wrap) or a minimum amount, whichever is greater; a product's fee category may differ from the category shoppers see, and media items carry an additional closing fee. Source: *Selling on Amazon fees* (https://sell.amazon.com/pricing). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-9.** Amazon lists FBA costs as fulfillment (based on the product's price, weight and dimensions), monthly storage by daily average cubic feet, aged inventory for items stored more than 181 days, returns processing, removal, disposal and liquidation per item, and inbound placement, and tells sellers to calculate margins with storage and fulfillment costs included. Source: *Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA)* (https://sell.amazon.com/fulfillment-by-amazon). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-10.** The SP-API Product Fees API returns, per SKU or ASIN at a caller-supplied listing price and fulfillment channel, a `TotalFeesEstimate` and a `FeeDetailList` of fee types and amounts; Amazon states these estimates are not guaranteed, and ASIN-based estimates use catalog size, which may differ from the item sent to Amazon. Sources: *Product Fees API* (https://developer-docs.amazon/sp-api/docs/product-fees-api.md); *productFeesV0.json* (https://github.com/amzn/selling-partner-api-models/blob/main/models/product-fees-api-model/productFeesV0.json). [official]

**P-goals-and-economics-11.** The FBA Fee Preview report (`GET_FBA_ESTIMATED_FBA_FEES_TXT_DATA`) holds per-SKU estimated referral, closing and fulfillment fees for active FBA offers, is updated at least once every 72 hours and can be requested once per day per seller; actual charges are returned by the Finances API's `listTransactions`, which may omit the last 48 hours. Sources: *Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA) Reports* (https://developer-docs.amazon/sp-api/docs/report-type-values-fba.md); *finances_2024-06-19.json* (https://github.com/amzn/selling-partner-api-models/blob/main/models/finances-api-model/finances_2024-06-19.json). Sources: Cooprider and Nassiri (https://www.amazon.science/blog/the-science-of-price-experiments-in-the-amazon-store); Jain, Hut, Islam and Pan (2023) (https://www.amazon.science/publications/cross-unit-spillovers-in-a-b-testing-empirical-evidence-from-ads). [research]

**P-goals-and-economics-69.** Jonathan Hershaff, a seller, reports a daily on/off switchback of his Amazon ads over about a month. It gave an incremental ROAS of 1.25 on total sales against a platform ROAS of about 2.35, and he treats 1.25 as a floor because a daily switchback ignores carryover. Source: *Estimating Amazon Ad Incrementality for Sellers* (https://whatstheimpact.substack.com/p/estimating-amazon-ad-incrementality). [opinion]

**P-goals-and-economics-70.** Repricer.com (Colin Palin) and AMALYZE hold that price changes move a listing's Featured Offer status and conversion rate, and through them its ACoS. AMALYZE says repricing makes an ACoS target "a moving goalpost". Ad Badger says coupons lift conversion by about 5–10% whatever the discount size. Quartile reports a 15–20% TACoS reduction for coordinated ads and promotions. None of these pages publishes data or a method. Sources: https://www.repricer.com/blog/amazon-ppc-vs-repricing-buy-box-impact/; https://amalyze.com/blog/price-cart-repricer-ppc-impact; https://www.adbadger.com/blog/amazon-coupons/; https://www.quartile.com/blog/why-the-smartest-brands-on-amazon-are-rethinking-promotions-in-2026. ### Seat's positions (enter no skill)

- [seat] ACOS = CPC ÷ (conversion rate × average order value), so break-even CPC = average order value × margin × conversion rate, and Amazon's "net profit per sale > 0" test is the same as ACOS < margin.
- [seat] TACoS = ACOS × (attributed sales ÷ total sales). With one margin across units, profit after ads as a share of total sales = margin − TACoS.
- [seat] Ads Math's margin names only cost of goods sold. A margin that leaves out referral and FBA fees overstates break-even ACOS by those fees' share of price.
- [seat] The Dorfman–Steiner condition is equivalent to a marginal total-sales ROAS of 1 ÷ margin. Under diminishing returns, a campaign's average ACOS sits below the ACOS of its last dollar. Example: going from $1,000 at ROAS 3.2 to $1,600 at ROAS 2.8 adds $1,280 of sales on $600, an incremental ROAS of about 2.13.
- [seat] Bid-from-ACoS formulas hold on paid CPC. Paid CPC equals the bid whenever the bid is under the soft reserve, so the bid–CPC ratio a seller observes decides how far a formula's target is met.
- [seat] `costPerNewToBrandPurchase` charges all spend to NTB purchases. It is an upper bound on the cost of a new customer, not that cost.
- [seat] Halo sales (`salesOtherSku7d`) carry the margins of the SKUs that sold, not of the advertised SKU.
- [seat] For a SKU being cleared, the comparison is ad cost per unit against the per-unit cost of removal, disposal, liquidation or continued storage and aged-inventory surcharge, not against margin.
- [seat] Ad-driven units shorten days of supply one for one. Stock cover and the ad budget are therefore one decision when cover is short, and capacity limits cap the replenishment that could relieve it.
- [seat] A Sponsored Products campaign has no goal field in Ads API v1. Its purpose exists only as the seller's label, outside the campaign object (the seller's words, a portfolio name, notes).
- [seat] Attributed sales are gross of returns, so spend over kept revenue is ACOS ÷ (1 − return rate on revenue). A per-unit fee change of Δ moves break-even ACOS by Δ ÷ price. A JP points rate p lowers break-even ACOS on the full price by p.
- [seat] With price at its own optimum, the Dorfman–Steiner ratio of ad spend to sales equals advertising elasticity ÷ |price elasticity|. The cheaper lever for the same extra sales depends on the ratio of the two elasticities, neither of which a seller's own regression of sales on spend or on price recovers without randomised variation.
